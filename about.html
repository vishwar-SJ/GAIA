Engineering & Conservation Dossier

About Project GAIA

An autonomous, end-to-end forest surveillance and early intervention network developed by the student branch of Sri Sai Ram Engineering College, Chennai, in partnership with EPICS in IEEE, Kenneth Anderson Nature Society (KANS), and the Tamil Nadu Forest Department.

Historical Background

Born Out of Urgent Need in Mathigiri Forest

The Mathigiri Forest and Cauvery North Wildlife Sanctuary in Hosur, India represent critical biodiversity corridors along the Eastern and Western Ghats. In recent years, catastrophic ecological events and human-wildlife crises severely threatened both the ecosystem and surrounding rural hamlets.

In January 2020, a devastating midnight forest fire swept across the Cauvery Wildlife Sanctuary. Due to nighttime darkness, rugged terrain, and the total absence of automated detection, the fire spread uncontrollably for hours before forest rangers could identify the blaze. Thousands of square meters of pristine flora and fauna habitat were consumed.

Compounding these fires were grave wildlife intrusions: a leopard repeatedly penetrated rural human settlements, killing 23 domestic animals (cattle, stray dogs, goats) and severely attacking 5 humans. Concurrently, herds of 37 elephants frequently crossed buffer zones, destroying crops and endangering farmers.

System Anatomy

How the GAIA Network Operates

GAIA functions as an interconnected, multi-layered mesh deployed directly in remote wilderness conditions without requiring mains power or wired communication.

1. Tree Canopy Nodes
Mounted high in tree canopies to monitor atmospheric triggers: RAK1906 (BME680) temperature, relative humidity, barometric pressure, VOC gas resistance, ambient light (RAK12020), and flame/smoke signatures.
2. Subterranean Ground Nodes
Embedded into root layers: RAK12023 + RAK12035 soil moisture & temperature sensors, RAK12005 precipitation detector, and RAK12015 vibration sensors to catch ground tremors from footsteps, vehicles, or falling timber.
3. Edge AI Vision Nodes
Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W / Pi 4 Model B interfaced with Sony IMX AI Camera modules. Captures 15-minute scheduled or PIR motion-triggered clips and executes local neural inference to classify fire and smoke hazards.
4. Long-Range LoRaWAN Mesh
Transmits encrypted telemetry across the IN865 band (16 channels) with dual 5.8 dBi high-gain antennas to a central RAK Gateway, providing continuous coverage spanning over 8 kilometers in rugged topography.
5. Cloud & Ranger Alert Portal
Data received at the gateway is routed via 4G LTE Airtel backhaul into The Things Stack cloud server and NGO web dashboard. Forest rangers receive real-time telemetry, location coords, and intrusion alarms.
6. 3D Digital Twining Simulation
3D point-cloud LiDAR/photogrammetry models of forest topology calculate optimal camera elevations and angle vectors to capture diverse wildlife classes rather than relying on human guesswork.

Custom Edge AI Classifier (Vishwar S J)

Unlike conventional IoT that transmits bandwidth-heavy raw video over cellular, GAIA utilizes lightweight on-device vision intelligence. The machine learning pipeline was trained across four distinct classes:

  1. Fire Only: Active open flame signatures.
  2. Smoke Only: Pre-combustion or smoldering plumes before visible fire spreads.
  3. Fire and Smoke: Full blaze conditions with heavy smoke development.
  4. No Fire: Normal dense forest canopy baseline.

Trained across 120 epochs using an unbiased 70:30 dataset split, achieving 80.69% accuracy. Saved as best.pt (PyTorch) and compiled into optimized .imx format for zero-overhead inference on Sony IMX AI Camera hardware.

WisBlock Sensor Suite (Thivyadharshini)

Each distributed node incorporates modular RAK WisBlock peripherals carefully selected for multi-parameter environmental acquisition:

  • RAK1906 (BME680): Temp, relative humidity, pressure, and gas index.
  • RAK12033 (IIM42652): 6-axis accelerometer & gyro for tree sway & seismic monitoring.
  • RAK12020: Ambient light intensity measurement.
  • RAK12006: Passive Infrared (PIR) motion detection for animal/human intrusion.
  • RAK12015: Vibration disturbance detector for chainsaw/illegal logging detection.
  • RAK12500: u-blox GNSS for precision node geolocation.
  • RAK12023 + RAK12035: Soil moisture and subterranean temperature sensing.
  • RAK12005 + RAK12030: Rain detection system.
  • RAK18001: Buzzer module for audible deterrent and diagnostic testing.

Power System & BMS Design (Kanmanni)

Operating in impenetrable jungle requires uncompromising power efficiency and safety. Kanmanni designed a dual-tier power architecture:

Raspberry Pi Nodes:

Powered by a 2S LiFePO4 (6V, 6Ah, 32650 cells) pack. LiFePO4 offers ultra-stable voltage discharge (7.2V down to ~5.5V), superior thermal stability in forest fires, 2000+ cycle life, and low internal resistance. Regulated via an MP1584 3A DC-DC buck converter (90–95% efficiency, 5V regulated output) and protected with a 1.5A slow-blow fuse.

Autonomous RAK WisBlock Nodes:

Powered by Saft 3.6V 2.6Ah Lithium Thionyl Chloride (Li-SOCl2) non-rechargeable chemistry, featuring negligible self-discharge and wide temperature tolerance for multi-year maintenance-free lifespan, stepped down with XL4015 converters.

// POWER ARCHITECTURE METRICS
Battery Chemistry: 2S LiFePO4 (32650) / Li-SOCl2
Energy Capacity: ~38 Wh (6V, 6Ah)
Cycle Life: 2000+ Full Cycles
DC Buck Converter: MP1584 (3A peak, 95% eff)
Protection: 1.5A Slow-Blow Thermal Fuse
Duty Cycling: Deep Sleep + Event Trigger

LoRaWAN Long-Range Mesh Topology

Operating in the IN865 Band (India 865–867 MHz) across 16 frequency channels, GAIA solves the notorious forest attenuation issue where foliage absorbs 2.4GHz Wi-Fi and Bluetooth.

  • OTAA Activation: Over-The-Air Activation ensures end-to-end cryptographic keys for secure packet transport.
  • High-Gain Dual Antennas: Dual 5.8 dBi omnidirectional antennas extend coverage to nearly 8 km in hilly forest terrain.
  • Airtel 4G Cellular Backhaul: Forward packets from the gateway to The Things Stack cloud server for processing.
  • Adaptive Spreading Factor: Spreading factors (SF7 through SF12) adapt dynamically to forest canopy jitter and seasonal foliage density.
